# LAB
## A Working Thesis
### Version 2.0

## Design doesn't happen in one medium.

The things we make are rarely the product of a single discipline.

A smart speaker is simultaneously an electronic device, an industrial design object, an assembly of manufactured components, a graphical interface, a textile product, and a piece of packaging.

A publication combines typography, imagery, materials, binding, printing, and physical construction. A clothing collection brings together fabric, patternmaking, graphic design, embroidery, hardware, and manufacturing. An exhibition might encompass all of these things within an architectural space.

Each is a unified creative work composed of many different materials, techniques, processes, and decisions.

Yet the software we use to design these things is largely organized around individual mediums.

We have sophisticated tools for vector graphics, 3D modeling, typography, electronics, textiles, manufacturing, and countless other specialties. Each is capable within its domain, but the larger creative project is fragmented across applications, files, and workflows.

We have extraordinarily sophisticated tools for designing the parts, but comparatively few for designing the whole.

## The most interesting work happens at the intersections.

A material, component, or design decision cannot always be evaluated independently of its surroundings.

A floor may be beautiful. A wall may be beautiful. A molding may be beautiful. But the quality of the room emerges from their relationships: where they meet, how their proportions interact, how they're constructed, and how they're experienced together.

The same is true at almost any scale.

The typography on a watch dial relates to its hands, case, and movement. The construction of a package influences its graphics, proportions, and materials. An embroidered patch behaves differently depending on the fabric beneath it.

These relationships are where practical constraints emerge, unexpected opportunities appear, and distinctive ideas develop.

Good design isn't simply the accumulation of well-designed parts. It's the successful integration of those parts into something coherent, useful, and compelling.

We believe creative software should make these relationships easier to explore.

## From designing files to designing things.

The basic unit of creative software has traditionally been a document: an image, a vector drawing, a CAD model, a pattern, a schematic.

But the thing we're trying to make is rarely any one of those documents.

LAB begins with the thing itself.

An object, a collection, a product, a room, or an entire exhibition can become the organizing structure for the creative work that goes into it.

Within that project, geometry, artwork, materials, components, references, and production information remain connected.

A package can be examined as an assembled three-dimensional object, unfolded into a dieline, edited as graphic artwork, and prepared for printing and cutting. These aren't separate projects. They're different representations of the same thing.

A designer can work at the scale of an entire collection, then move into the details of a single component without losing the larger context.

The goal isn't to replace every specialized creative application. It's to create an environment in which their disciplines can work together.

## A studio, not just a canvas.

The creative process rarely begins with a finished specification.

It begins with references, experiments, fragments, materials, conversations, and things we want to try.

LAB treats these as part of the work.

Its spatial environment is inspired by the physical studio table: a shared surface where drawings, photographs, objects, material samples, prototypes, and unfinished ideas can coexist.

A project might contain several versions of a lamp, a collection of fabric swatches, a drawing of a mechanical component, photographs of historical references, and notes about how something should feel.

All of these can remain visible, arranged, compared, and revisited.

Ideas don't need to disappear into folders or be flattened into a single final document.

Iterations and variants can exist side by side. An abandoned direction can remain available. A reference can become the starting point for something new.

The environment should support the way people actually think and work, not simply the way software organizes files.

## Three interconnected creative environments.

LAB brings together three fundamental aspects of making physical things.

### Form
*What something is, how it looks, and how it is composed.*

Form is the environment for visual and spatial design.

It brings together typography, vector graphics, imagery, 3D geometry, materials, color, surface treatments, and composition.

A designer can explore an object's proportions, arrange a collection, develop packaging artwork, design a garment graphic, or work with the details of a physical assembly.

Two-dimensional and three-dimensional design belong to the same creative context.

### Function
*How something works, fits together, moves, and behaves.*

Function is the environment for understanding relationships and behavior.

Dimensions, constraints, mechanisms, components, connections, and interactions become part of the design.

A clock needs a movement. A lamp needs electrical components. A package needs a closure. A garment needs seams and a construction that can be assembled.

Function connects creative intention with the practical realities of the object.

It can incorporate specialist engineering work without requiring LAB to replace every engineering discipline.

### Fabricate
*How something is produced, assembled, and brought into the physical world.*

Fabricate connects the design to the processes used to make it.

Printing, cutting, folding, sewing, embroidery, 3D printing, machining, and other methods become available within the project.

The environment can understand materials, equipment, production constraints, and the instructions needed to manufacture an object.

A design can become a printed sheet, a sewing pattern, a toolpath, an embroidery program, or an assembly of manufactured components.

These three environments aren't isolated stages in a linear workflow.

They're different perspectives on the same evolving project.

A change in construction may influence appearance. A material decision may change how something is manufactured. A production limitation may inspire a different design.

The relationships remain visible throughout.

## Intelligence belongs in the creative process.

We believe AI changes what's possible in creative software.

Traditionally, learning a new discipline meant learning a new application, its vocabulary, conventions, and technical requirements.

An intelligent creative environment can help bridge those disciplines.

Someone might begin with a simple request: "I want to make a notebook."

LAB can help explore what kind of notebook, understand its components, propose materials and construction methods, and establish an editable starting point.

A more experienced designer might arrive with exact specifications, photographs, measurements, or an existing model.

Both can work in the same environment.

The AI collaborator can research references, generate initial constructions, identify missing information, explore alternatives, and operate the tools available within the project.

But the result shouldn't be limited to something generated by AI.

It should become something the designer can directly manipulate.

A generated object can expose dimensions, materials, components, and meaningful controls. Its artwork can be edited with professional design tools. Its construction can be refined and tested.

The intelligence used to create something becomes part of a continuing creative process rather than a single generation event.

And that process has memory.

The project retains its references, iterations, decisions, assumptions, and unresolved questions. A designer can return to an earlier idea, find a forgotten reference, or continue a conversation about work developed weeks before.

AI isn't a replacement for creative judgment. It's a way to extend the range of what a person can explore, understand, and make.

## The real world is the point.

LAB is organized around physical things.

Dimensions are measured in inches, millimeters, and centimeters, not simply pixels.

Materials have thickness, texture, weight, and physical properties. Components occupy space. Objects have clearances, connections, and construction methods.

A design can begin as an approximation and become more precisely defined as the work progresses.

A conceptual model may be sufficient to explore a silhouette. A physical prototype requires meaningful dimensions. A production-ready object needs a more complete understanding of its materials, construction, and manufacturing requirements.

LAB should support that progression without demanding that everything be resolved at the beginning.

It should also make uncertainties visible.

What do we know? What have we assumed? What remains to be measured, selected, tested, or fabricated?

These questions are part of designing, not administrative work that happens afterward.

## Making is part of designing.

Digital design and physical production have often been treated as separate activities.

We design something in one environment, export a file, move into another application, prepare it for a machine or manufacturer, and eventually discover how the object behaves in the real world.

LAB aims to bring these activities closer together.

The tools, materials, and processes available to a designer should inform the work from the beginning.

A folding package can be developed alongside its cutting pattern. An embroidered graphic can be evaluated through its actual stitch construction. A 3D-printed object can be considered in relation to its material, orientation, and fabrication constraints.

And the relationship should work in both directions.

A physical prototype may reveal a problem, suggest an improvement, or inspire an entirely different approach.

That knowledge should return to the project, becoming part of its ongoing development.

Fabrication isn't simply the final step. It's another way of thinking, experimenting, and making creative decisions.

## A more complete creative environment.

We imagine a future in which the boundaries between creative disciplines become easier to cross.

Where someone can begin with an idea and explore it through whatever combination of visual design, geometry, engineering, materials, and manufacturing the project requires.

Where sophisticated capabilities are available without requiring every person to become an expert in every tool.

Where designers can move freely between individual details and the complete work.

And where the creative environment understands not just what something looks like, but what it is, how its parts relate, and how it might become real.

The ambition isn't to eliminate specialized knowledge or the tools that embody it.

It's to make that knowledge more accessible, more connected, and more useful throughout the creative process.

**We believe the next generation of creative software should be organized around what we're making, not just the mediums we use to make it.**

LAB is our exploration of what that could look like.

**An integrated creative environment for imagining, designing, developing, and making physical things.**
